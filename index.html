Flip Anything with Agentic Robotics Self-Learning

Shuangyu Xie1, Kaiyuan Chen1, Ken Goldberg1
1University of California, Berkeley
Paper Code Videos Results

TL;DR

Two YAM arms must turn an industrial hand tool face down on a mat and let go, using only static RGB-D cameras and the joint encoders (four cameras in simulation). A coding agent builds the policy from scratch as ordinary, inspectable code and improves it in a self-learning loop: run 360 simulated episodes, group the failures by mechanism, change the source, and regression-test. Over 36 versions the agent added 20 motion primitives in 5 families, combined into 4 flip strategies, and took the tune success rate from 24.7 % to 95.0 %. Nothing is learned inside the policy; what is learned lives in the code.

Real-world experiment

  • Same arms, different eyes. The real bench uses the two YAM arms from simulation, but sees the scene through one overhead ZED Mini and a D405 wrist camera instead of four static cameras.
  • Same policy code. The code learned in simulation runs on the rig through a bridge that adds safety checks.
  • Not solved yet. Real runs fail in ways the simulator rarely produced. The last section shows a failure and what the gap is made of.

Task Setup

FlipAnything: 24 hand tools, each spawned face up at a random pose on a gridded mat in front of a bimanual YAM rig. One policy has to flip all of them, without being told which tool it is looking at.

  • Robot. Two 6-DoF YAM arms with parallel grippers. Action: 14 joint and gripper targets at 50 Hz.
  • Sensing in simulation. RGB and depth from four fixed cameras (top, bench, close, profile) plus joint and finger encoders. No wrist camera. The real rig differs; see Perception.
  • Never read. Object pose, meshes, tool names, segmentation masks or the score.
  • Success. Face down (cos ≤ −0.9), on the mat, at rest, released, both hands clear, held for 1.5 s, all within 80 s.
  • Splits. Tune: 24 tools × episodes 0–14 (360). Held-out: episodes 15–19 (120), one registered run per frozen version.

The Self-Learning Loop

Each iteration is a frozen snapshot evaluated on all 360 tune episodes. The agent reads the policy's own event log, groups the failures by the first mechanism recorded (no plan, lost grip, squeeze-out, receiving-hand pinch, false "done", lost track), writes a probe for the largest group, changes the code and evaluates again. Every version is kept, including the regressions. Run-to-run spread on identical code is about ±1.4 points.

Six phases of learning

  1. v001 → v005 · 24.7 → 80.6 %Build the mechanisms. 235 of 271 early failures were "no plan". The agent added in-hand ICP registration, then stand and topple for compact parts, then a strategy cascade.
  2. v006 → v011 · 73.9 → 81.4 %Make the pinch hold. Pads move to the widest band, gentle release, verified B pinch. Three regressions, each a grasp rule that removed one tool's only grasp.
  3. v012 → v016 · 86.9 → 89.4 %Reach 90 %. Push look-ahead, bridged sections, fingertip touch-off and a guarded-descent fix. v016 frozen: 90.8 % held-out, 88.3 % under sensor noise.
  4. v017 → v024 · 89–91 %Plateau. Test lift, reposition, slow roll, partial-turn finish, face-normal placement, B deepen. Each moves one or two tools; the total stays inside the noise.
  5. v025 → v030 · 91.9 %Graph-as-Policy audit. The loop was re-expressed as a GaP workflow. Same score, but per-node failure attribution pointed at the next two levers.
  6. v033 → v036 · 94.7 → 95.0 %Toward 98 %. A guarded-contact pinch takes the 5 mm scissors from 0 to 13 of 15; a face check after drops removes most false "face down" beliefs.

Learned Motion Primitives

The policy flips a tool with one of four strategies, chosen per observation from geometry and reachability, with both hand assignments searched. The strategies are built from 20 motion primitives. The chip on each primitive is the version where the self-learning loop added it or made it work; unmarked ones were in the first version.

claw hammer · 27 s

1 · In-air handover v001

Hand A pinches top-down, lifts and rolls 90°. Hand B pinches the far face, A releases, and B rolls back and sets the tool down face down. Used for 19 of 24 tools.

pipe wrench · 39 s

2 · Reposition, then handover v017

When no handover is reachable, A carries the tool to the middle of the mat, rotating it if needed. The target pose is chosen by planning the handover on the predicted scene.

nylon lock nut · 21 s

3 · Stand, then topple v003

For compact parts. A pivots the part about its bottom edge onto its side, past the tipping angle atan(W/H) read from the height map. B pushes it the rest of the way over.

multimeter · 16 s

4 · Push-stand, then topple v005

For parts too wide to pinch. Two arc pushes with closed fingertips and no grasp. Since v012 the first direction is chosen so that the second push is also reachable.

Checks learned from failures

Most of the gain after v010 came from runtime checks wrapped around the primitives. Each turns a drop into a retry.

Verify before letting go

B's pinch is checked from the finger encoder before A releases (v010); A's grip after closing (v011). A width narrower than the planned section sends B 8 mm deeper (v024).

Catch slips early

A 1 cm test lift catches a pinch squeezing out before the full lift (v017). The part is set down and re-planned instead of dropped mid-air.

Know the outcome

A tool stopped part-way through a topple is finished with another push (v021). After a drop, the visible top is aligned against the first-seen top to tell up from down (v036).

Perception Pipeline

Four fixed RGB-D cameras, no wrist camera and no learned models. Every step is explicit geometry: filtering, RANSAC, connected components, PCA and ICP. The pipeline runs every time the policy re-observes: at the start, after each stage, and when checking a grasp or a placement.

Capture RGB-D

  • Segmentation is never requested. Optional robustness noise: depth noise ∝ z², dropout, per-camera extrinsic error.

  • Drops pixels more than 2 cm from their neighbourhood median.

Back-project and fuse

pinhole → world frame
  • Every 2nd pixel, camera-to-world transform, all 4 clouds merged with colour and source camera. ROI x 0.30–0.92 m, y ±0.40 m.
perception.py · backproject()
4

Mask out the robot

forward kinematics + collision spheres
  • Both arms posed from the joint encoders; points within 18 mm of any collision primitive removed.
perception.py · robot_mask()
5

Fit the support plane

sequential RANSAC + least squares
  • Up to 3 near-horizontal planes; the highest large one is the mat. One plane per camera absorbs that camera's calibration offset.
  • If the cameras disagree by more than 2 mm, a fingertip touch-off measures the true height.
perception.py · fit_table()
6

Segment the object

height threshold + 3-D connected components
  • Points from max(3 mm, 3σ) to 30 cm above the plane, 8 mm voxels, 26-connected. Largest cluster is the object; occlusion splits are merged back.
perception.py · segment_object()
7

Describe the object

2.5-D height map + PCA footprint
  • Max height and top colour on a 4 mm grid; the unseen underside is assumed to reach the table. PCA gives axes, extents, centre and height.
perception.py · HeightMap, describe()

Grasp candidates

Pinch lines every 8 mm and 15° of yaw, kept when the section fits the 94 mm gripper. Pads at the widest band; wedges rejected; ranked by gravity stability.

cost = 0.6·|d_b| + 1.2·|d_c|·(3 cm / w)
grasp.py · stability_cost()

Which face is up

A yaw sweep plus 2-D ICP aligns the first-seen top surface to the current one. Footprint overlap, height RMS and colour difference decide up, side or down.

faces.py

In-hand pose at handover

Trimmed point-to-point ICP registers the pick-time model onto the points around the held tool (40 iterations, 12 mm trim). A corrects its pose or B re-plans.

perception.py · icp()

Statics, not physics

No mass or friction is estimated. Tipping angle and slide-vs-tip come from the perceived shape.

Failure Modes and the Sim-to-Real Gap

In simulation the policy reaches 95 %. On the real bench it fails far more often, and in different ways. This section lists what still fails in simulation, measures how the real rig differs, and reports what happened when those differences were put back into the simulator for another round of self-learning.

Still failing in simulation (v036, 18 of 360)

  • Resting just short of face down. ESP32 board at cos −0.88 (3) and box cutter propped at −0.83 (2), against the −0.9 threshold.
  • Drops. Measuring tape after drops (3), scissors dropped in the roll (2).
  • Receiving pinch. Power drill (2): hand B cannot close on the far face.
  • Singles. Pipe wrench (2), hammer, pliers, screwdriver and tape measure (1 each).

What the gap is made of

Each difference was modelled as a physical mechanism in the simulator and checked against a first-principles prediction. No rate was fitted to the real failures.

Soft arm

The real joints run a PD controller at kp 80/80/80/40/10/10, against 300 on every joint in the old twin. The hand sags 12 mm under a 0.47 kg load (real: 10 mm).

Force-limited gripper

A blocked real grip holds about 50 N whatever gap is commanded. The old twin held 18 N.

Pinch ride-up

With a soft arm (about 2.3 mm/N at the fingertips), a 50 N squeeze on a rounded section above its equator lifts the hand over the part. This explains the higher closes and the misses on round or thin parts.

Soft foam mat

The EVA mat lets a pressing fingertip sink 2–5 mm before it stiffens. The old twin's mat gave 0.4 mm.

Real objects

Round rubber handles modelled as curved sleeves, not one convex hull. The spring clamp as two halves on a sprung pivot, not one rigid body.

Sensors and cameras

Stereo depth artefacts, rig calibration error, encoder offsets, gripper reading error and one step of latency. The real rig also has different cameras (see Perception).

Self-learning again, in the gap simulator

About 250 episodes over 10 hours on the rig's own code path, with the clamp, tape roll and folding tool at their real positions. A new primitive counted only if removing it lost episodes.

  1. 0 of 4 new primitives were effective. Hold-z close, scoop push, push-through flip and a B drop guard each came out within one episode of their ablation on the same seeds.
  2. Ride-up is real but not the limit. Cancelling it did not change clamp success.
  3. The folding tool is bounded by kinematics. No handover is reachable, and a pinch-stand needs 79° of wrist pitch where the arm reaches 25–65°.
  4. The clamp is a perception problem. 7 of 10 clamp failures end face up while the overhead face check reads "unknown". A close look with the wrist camera did not separate the faces either.
